By Marvellous Chuyuoh, Founder of Zenphry

A company announces a new way of handling work. An employee asks, “What does this mean for the customer request already on my desk?” The manager has the launch presentation, but it does not answer that question.

A useful change communication plan helps managers explain what changes in everyday work and gives employees a reliable route for questions. It should identify the audience, message, messenger, timing and follow-up responsibility. The plan needs to cover the conversation after the announcement as carefully as the announcement itself.

Prepare managers for the questions they will receive

Gallup’s May 2024 report on a nationally representative survey of 18,665 U.S. employees found that leaders and managers were more likely than individual contributors to report extensive disruptive change. It also reported associations between effective leadership communication and employee engagement during disruption. Those findings do not establish that one communication technique causes better outcomes. [1]

The practical implication is to give managers room to understand a change before asking them to explain it. In a newer, AI-specific example, Gallup reported in August 2026 that half of 102 participants in its Global CHRO Roundtable survey lacked confidence in managers’ ability to guide employees’ AI use. That sample should not be treated as a percentage of all employers. [2]

Hold a manager briefing early enough to surface missing decisions. Ask each manager to describe what the change means for an ordinary task in their team. If two managers give conflicting answers, resolve the difference or explicitly record the uncertainty.

Create a short brief that can be used in conversation

For each affected group, prepare a brief with the following information. A small business can keep this in a shared document rather than creating a new communication platform.

  • The business problem and the reason for addressing it now.
  • The tasks or decisions that will change for this group.
  • The responsibilities that remain the same.
  • The first action employees need to take and when it applies.
  • The practice, guidance and time available to help them.
  • The questions still unresolved, with an owner and next update date.

Select a channel people can actually use. An email may provide a reference for office staff; a team briefing and accessible written summary may work better for colleagues who rarely check email during a shift. Give absent employees a way to receive the same information and ask questions.

Explain the effect on a real piece of work

Imagine a fictional business introducing an approval step for changes to customer project scope. The manager could say:

“For new scope requests from Monday, record the requested change in the project workspace before promising a delivery date. The project lead will review it. Requests already agreed with customers keep their current commitments. We will practise one example together today. We have not yet settled the route for urgent requests while the lead is away; the operations manager will confirm that before the new process starts.”

This is an illustrative script, not a Zenphry client story. It gives employees a task, a boundary and a visible unresolved decision. It avoids promising that every concern has already been settled.

Ask a colleague to explain what they would do with a sample request. Invite them to identify where the instructions fail. That gives the manager specific information to improve the brief, rather than relying on a room of silent nods.

Investigate concerns before choosing a response

“This will not work” can mean several things. Ask for the step or situation behind the concern. A missing permission needs an access fix. Duplicate entry needs a process decision. Unclear expectations need a better explanation. Concern about a changing role needs an honest conversation with the appropriate leader.

If someone says the new process adds work, ask them to show where. Record the extra step and take it to the person who can change it. Do not promise that all feedback will be accepted. Explain which decisions remain open and how the team will hear the outcome.

When the answer is unknown, say so plainly: “I do not have a confirmed answer. I have raised it with the process owner and will update you on Thursday, even if the decision is still pending.” A promised update is within the manager’s control; a promised decision may not be.

Close the loop after the announcement

Keep a question record containing the issue, affected task, owner, status and next update. Remove personal details when sharing themes with the wider team. If several people ask the same question, revise the instructions in the place everyone uses rather than answering each person separately forever.

After the first week of use, ask which instruction was hardest to follow and what workaround people needed. Review the answers with the process owner. Publish the changes made and explain decisions that remain unchanged. Choose the review timing to match how often the task occurs.

A conversation for your next team meeting

Choose one change already announced. Ask, “Which part is still unclear when you apply it to your own work?” Give the team time to answer, then assign an owner to the most consequential unresolved question.
