By Marvellous Chuyuoh, Founder of Zenphry

A launch date can be agreed long before a team is ready to work differently. Someone may still be unsure who approves an exception. Training may overlap with a customer deadline. A new form may ask for information that another department does not collect.

A change readiness assessment examines whether the people affected have the understanding, time, skills and support to use a proposed change. For an owner or operations leader introducing a new process, its useful output is a decision about what needs to happen before rollout.

Prosci describes readiness assessment as a way to examine organizational preparedness and tailor the support needed for a particular change. Its guidance includes leadership commitment and employee readiness, rather than technical preparation alone. [1] The practical review below is a suggested starting point for a small business, not a validated assessment or a guarantee of success.

Describe the change through one real task

Choose a task that will be different after launch. Write down how it works today and walk through the proposed approach with someone who actually performs it. Use a completed, suitably anonymized example rather than an idealized flowchart.

For a new customer intake process, follow an inquiry from arrival to assignment. Check where the information comes from, who decides whether it is complete and what happens when an urgent request arrives. This makes the change specific enough to assess.

Ask for evidence behind each readiness answer

Bring the process owner, a manager and representatives of the affected roles into the review. Ask the following questions and record what supports each answer.

  • Purpose: Can the people doing the task explain the problem the change is intended to solve?
  • Responsibilities: Can they identify who owns the next step and who resolves an exception?
  • Practice: Can they complete a realistic example with the proposed instructions and access?
  • Capacity: What existing work competes with preparation, and what time has been set aside?
  • Support: Who will answer questions after launch, and can that person actually provide the help?
  • Follow through: Who will review early problems and decide what to adjust?

Record each answer as demonstrated, partly demonstrated or not yet demonstrated. Attach a short observation. “Training sent” provides less useful evidence than “the coordinator completed a sample request but could not route an exception.” Avoid converting these observations into a precise readiness percentage without a defensible scoring method.

Check what else is landing on the same team

A team might be able to learn one process while struggling to absorb several at once. Prosci distinguishes the disruption caused by changes from an organization’s capacity to absorb them. Its 2026 discussion of change fatigue argues that counting initiatives alone does not capture that balance. [2]

Create a short calendar showing the affected team’s customer commitments, absences and other scheduled changes. Include preparation and support work, not only launch dates. If the calendar exposes a collision, decide whether to narrow the pilot, provide cover or move optional work. A fixed external deadline makes these tradeoffs more important.

Turn the review into a decision

Consider a fictional service business moving customer requests from email into a shared intake queue. This is an illustrative example, not a Zenphry client case.

In a rehearsal, the coordinator can create a request and assign it. However, urgent requests have no named backup owner, and the manager who normally answers questions will be away during launch week. The review identifies two specific gaps rather than labeling the whole team resistant.

A useful decision record would say: “Pilot standard requests with the available coordinator. The operations lead will name and brief a backup before urgent requests enter the queue. Review unresolved requests with the team before extending the pilot.” This records scope, ownership and a condition for expansion. It does not assume the pilot has already worked.

For each unresolved item, specify the action, owner, due date and evidence needed to close it. If a gap could prevent essential work from being completed, resolve it before expanding the change. For less consequential gaps, agree what support and monitoring will make a limited pilot reasonable.

Questions to settle before rollout

When should the assessment happen? Run an initial review while scope and support can still change. Repeat the relevant checks when a rehearsal, staffing change or revised requirement alters the picture.

Who decides whether to proceed? Name a business owner with authority over the affected work. Technical readiness and employee feedback should inform that decision; neither should disappear into an average score.

What should the team leave with? A clear pilot or rollout decision, unresolved actions and the date of the next review. If the assessment produces a report without owners, translate its findings into decisions before moving on.

Start with your next operational change

Choose one upcoming process change and ask a colleague to demonstrate the hardest handoff. Use what you observe to decide where preparation is still needed.
