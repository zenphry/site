A practical guide for business owners and operations leaders

The licenses are purchased. The launch email is sent. The training session is complete.

On Monday morning, someone still has to answer a practical question: “How am I supposed to do my work differently?”

That question deserves a place in every AI rollout.

AI adoption involves changes to responsibilities, decisions, handoffs and working habits. Change management helps people understand those changes, prepare for them and get support as they put them into practice.

For business owners and operations leaders, that means looking beyond access to the tool and examining the work surrounding it.

Why this conversation matters now

Gallup’s workplace AI research, updated September 30, 2026, identifies manager support and workflow integration as two conditions strongly associated with frequent AI use. These findings describe relationships, rather than proving that either condition alone causes better results. They do, however, give leaders useful areas to examine. [1]

Here is a practical starting point for your next rollout.

Prepare the work and the people

1. Choose one task people recognize

“Use AI to improve productivity” leaves a lot for employees to interpret.

Start with a specific task: preparing a weekly update, drafting an internal handoff or organizing meeting actions. Ask the people doing that work where time is lost and what makes the output useful. Their answers should shape the change.

2. Explain what will change

Consider a team introducing an approved AI tool to help draft project updates. Before the rollout, agree on the information the tool can use, who checks the draft, who approves it and where the final update belongs.

Make responsibility explicit. A draft can be generated quickly while still requiring careful review.

3. Give managers something practical

A manager needs more than a presentation announcing the new approach. Prepare a short guide covering:

  • The task the team will try.
  • An example of an acceptable result.
  • The checks required before using that result.
  • Where questions and problems should go.
  • When the team will review its experience.

Give managers time to practise, ask questions and identify problems before expecting them to guide everyone else.

Responsibilities. Support. Adoption.

Define the work. Prepare the team. Review what changes.

Test, learn and support adoption

4. Test with the people who will use it

Imagine a small consulting team piloting AI-assisted internal handoffs. This is an illustrative example, not a Zenphry client case.

The team selects an approved tool and uses information permitted under its policies. AI drafts a handoff summary. The project owner checks it against the source information. The receiving colleague flags anything unclear.

During the pilot, the team records missing details, corrections and time spent reviewing. If drafting becomes faster but checking becomes harder, that is useful information. The workflow needs adjustment before wider use.

5. Measure whether the work improves

Training attendance tells you who attended. Login counts tell you who opened the tool. For this example, useful measures could include total preparation and review time, corrections required and follow-up questions from the receiving team.

Record a baseline before the pilot. Compare similar tasks afterwards, and ask employees what still feels difficult. Agree in advance on what would justify expanding, revising or stopping the pilot. Success should include the quality of the work and the experience of the people doing it.

A conversation to have this week

Bring one manager and the people doing the selected task together. Ask: “What will change in this task, who needs support, and how will we know the new approach is helping?”

Write down the answers. Assign an owner to unresolved questions. Set a date to review what happens in practice.
