By Marvellous Chuyuoh, Founder of Zenphry

Your team has completed training and the new process is live. The next question is whether people can use it reliably and whether it improves the work.

Change adoption metrics describe how people use a new way of working. They should be paired with quality checks and a relevant business outcome. Start with a defined task, a clear group of eligible users or cases, and an agreed review period. Without those definitions, a rising number can be difficult to interpret.

Prosci’s measurement guidance distinguishes change-management activity, individual adoption and organizational performance. It also identifies adoption speed, utilization and proficiency as useful dimensions of individual change. [1] The example below applies those concepts through an original, simplified review for a business process. It is not a universal benchmark.

Define the behavior before choosing the metric

Suppose a business introduces a shared intake queue for customer requests. “Use the queue” is too broad. A more precise expectation is: “Every eligible new request is recorded in the queue, assigned an owner and given a next action before work begins.”

Agree which requests are eligible. For example, the pilot may cover one service team and exclude requests already in progress. Record those boundaries before counting results. If eligibility changes later, annotate the change instead of treating the new percentage as directly comparable.

Choose one accountable process owner, name the source of each measure and decide who will check data quality. Include the people who handle requests; they can explain exceptions that a report alone will miss.

Build a small scorecard around the work

Track four questions for the pilot. Was the new route used? Was the work completed correctly? Did a relevant business outcome change? What obstacles remain? Training attendance can sit alongside these measures as preparation evidence, but it should not substitute for observations of actual work.

The following figures are invented solely to demonstrate the calculations. They are not Zenphry results, client data, forecasts or performance targets.

  • Use: During the review week, 40 of 50 eligible requests entered the queue. Workflow use is 40 divided by 50, or 80 percent.
  • Quality: Of those 40 requests, 30 had both an owner and a next action recorded before work began. Completion quality among queue requests is 30 divided by 40, or 75 percent.
  • Combined coverage: The same 30 correctly handled requests represent 30 divided by all 50 eligible requests, or 60 percent. This prevents the quality percentage from concealing requests that bypassed the queue.
  • Business outcome: Median time from receipt to owner assignment moved from 12 hours in a baseline period to 9 hours in the pilot period. That is a 3-hour difference, or 25 percent of the baseline.

The final comparison does not establish that the process caused the improvement. Check whether request complexity, staffing, opening hours and case volume were comparable. Include unresolved requests separately so a faster completion figure does not hide a growing backlog.

Use the findings to choose the next action

In this fictional example, the owner should investigate the 10 requests outside the queue and the 10 queue requests missing required information. These are two different problems. Interview the people involved and inspect a few cases before deciding what support to provide.

If an intake channel bypasses the queue automatically, additional employee training may not address it. If the next-action field is misunderstood, a clearer example may help. If people cannot assign an owner because decision rights are unresolved, the process owner needs to make that decision.

Record one corrective action for each important cause and check the same measure at the next review. Avoid changing several definitions at once, which can make apparent improvement difficult to distinguish from a reporting change.

Set a review rhythm that matches the process

A weekly review may suit a frequent request-handling process. A monthly task may need several cycles before there is enough evidence to judge adoption. Pick a cadence based on opportunities to perform the task, the consequences of mistakes and the speed at which support is needed.

Review team-level trends together with employee feedback. Explain why information is being collected and use only the detail needed for the decision. Counting clicks or ranking employees by raw activity can distract from whether the task was completed well.

Agree what happens when results fall short. For example, continue a limited pilot while correcting a routing gap, or pause expansion if essential requests cannot be handled. Targets should reflect the business requirement and baseline; the fictional percentages above are not recommended thresholds.

Keep an owner after the project closes

Before the rollout team moves on, name who maintains the instructions, supports new starters and reviews recurring exceptions. Record where the current process lives and when it will next be reviewed. Revisit the measures after a staffing, system or policy change alters how the work is done.

What if use is high but outcomes are flat? Examine the process design and the original business assumption. Employees may be following the instructions correctly while the approach itself needs revision.
